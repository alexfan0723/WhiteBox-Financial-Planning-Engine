# From Expert Experience to Reviewable Questions: What a Planning Detector Can and Cannot Do

![Cue question review diagram](../../assets/cue-question-review.svg)

[简体中文](expert-experience-to-reviewable-questions.zh-CN.md)

Experienced planners often pause at a few details in a household plan. Property may represent a large share of wealth while liquid resources are thin. Income may appear stable while education, elder support, and insurance commitments arrive in the same period. A protection arrangement may reduce one risk while increasing pressure on long-term cash flow.

These observations come from experience, and they need to be explained. WhiteBox's public detector research explores how recurring expert cues can become reviewable questions. The goal is to make the basis, scope, and limits of a planning judgment easier to discuss.

## 1. Expert Experience Often Begins with a Cue

A cue is an observation that deserves closer attention. It is not a conclusion and it is not a recommendation. For example, a planner may notice that a household relies heavily on property, has several commitments due within a short period, or is using one income source to support many long-term responsibilities.

The cue matters because household planning combines facts that can interact across time. A balance sheet can look strong while its accessible cash flow is under pressure. A goal can look affordable today while becoming fragile when the timing of a mortgage, family support, or retirement changes.

## 2. A Cue Should Become a Clear Review Question

The useful next step is a question that someone can inspect. Does the household have accessible resources for the commitments that arrive first? Which assumptions about income, spending, or property liquidity support the current view? What would change if one responsibility started earlier or lasted longer?

Questions of this kind keep facts, assumptions, and open items visible. They also make it possible for an adviser and a household to disagree productively about priorities without treating an early interpretation as a settled answer.

## 3. Detector Research Organizes Recurring Questions

WhiteBox uses the term detector for an experimental research direction that organizes recurring expert questions into more repeatable review signals. A detector may identify a pattern worth examining, point to a possible missing fact, or suggest a boundary condition that needs testing.

It does not decide for a household. It does not turn a signal into advice. Similar signals can arise from different family structures, contractual arrangements, and life stages. A credible research process therefore tests whether a signal recurs, searches for counterexamples, and records where the signal may not apply.

## 4. Mainland China Context Changes the Questions

For many mainland China households, property, mortgages, education funding, elder support, insurance commitments, and retirement preparation are closely connected. The same balance-sheet ratio can carry different practical implications when a family faces a near-term repayment, a planned education expense, or limited ability to sell property.

Local professional judgment remains essential. It helps distinguish a useful warning signal from a reasonable household choice made under known constraints and priorities.

## 5. Expert Review Remains the Decision Boundary

The intended value of detector research is to make experience easier to inspect, discuss, and improve. Its output should leave the cue, the question, the assumptions, and the uncertainty visible for review.

Formal planning conclusions should still rest on confirmed facts, explicit assumptions, traceable analysis, and professional interpretation. A detector can help an expert see a question earlier. The expert remains responsible for deciding what the question means in context.
