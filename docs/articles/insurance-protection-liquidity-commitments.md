# Insurance in Household Planning: Keep Three Questions Separate

[简体中文](insurance-protection-liquidity-commitments.zh-CN.md)

Families often begin an insurance discussion with the coverage amount. Soon after, whether a policy can provide cash today and whether future premiums remain affordable get folded into the same answer. The numbers grow, while the household's accounts become less clear.

## Three questions worth separating

- Coverage asks what support the household may receive if the conditions in a policy are met. It cannot be treated as cash available today.

- Cash value or surrender value depends on the contract. Access, timing, and the consequences of using it all need separate review.

- Premiums are commitments that continue over time. They compete for future income with housing, education, elder support, and retirement preparation.

## A fictional household balance sheet

The example below is entirely fictional. All figures are in Chinese yuan and are used only to show how a household can organize the discussion.

- A couple with one child has annual after-tax income of CNY 480,000.

- The household holds CNY 300,000 in cash and money-market funds, plus CNY 500,000 in long-term investments. Its owner-occupied home is valued at CNY 4.8 million with CNY 2.1 million of mortgage debt. The home has substantial value, but routine spending still depends mainly on income and liquid cash.

- Necessary annual spending totals CNY 420,000.

  - Mortgage and housing costs total CNY 132,000.
  - Everyday living costs total CNY 144,000.
  - Education costs total CNY 60,000.
  - Elder-support and medical provision totals CNY 36,000.
  - Insurance premiums total CNY 48,000.

- In an ordinary year, the household retains a CNY 60,000 buffer after these costs.

- The illustrative policies include CNY 2 million of term-life coverage and CNY 1 million of critical-illness coverage. A separate contract shows CNY 120,000 of cash value. The coverage applies only when relevant conditions are met. The CNY 120,000 should not be added to the CNY 300,000 of liquid funds until the contract terms and consequences have been reviewed.

## Put the figures on a timeline

Paying a premium this year and being able to keep paying it for years are different questions. The household can examine two pressure cases.

- Income-interruption case

  Annual income falls by one quarter to CNY 360,000 while necessary spending remains CNY 420,000. The annual gap is CNY 60,000.

  If the family postpones CNY 24,000 of optional spending, it still needs CNY 36,000 from liquid funds. After one year, liquid funds fall from CNY 300,000 to CNY 264,000. Two years of the same conditions would reduce them to CNY 228,000, before considering investment returns, medical events, or other surprises.

- Earlier education-spending case

  If education spending rises by CNY 20,000, annual spending becomes CNY 440,000. With normal income, the household still retains CNY 40,000.

  If this increase arrives during the income interruption, income is CNY 360,000 and spending is CNY 440,000. The gap reaches CNY 80,000. Even after postponing CNY 24,000 of optional spending, CNY 56,000 must come from liquid funds.

The figures do not make a decision for the household. They identify the years and commitments that create pressure.

## How to place policy value in the plan

The illustrative CNY 120,000 of cash value can enter the conversation, but it should be recorded separately from coverage and immediately available cash.

- Does the contract allow access at the time it is needed?

- What changes to coverage or future premiums follow from access?

- Is the household filling a short-term gap or facing pressure that could last for years?

- If the value is used and premiums continue, what does the later cash-flow picture look like?

The household may decide to use, retain, or adjust the policy arrangement. Each option needs the available money, later premiums, and changed protection to be considered together.

## Make the discussion reviewable

WhiteBox's public research direction is to present confirmed facts, assumptions that remain open for discussion, and outcomes under different scenarios separately. This gives households and professionals a way to check which money is available, when protection may apply, and which years are most exposed to cash-flow pressure.

Once insurance is placed inside the household plan, the discussion becomes more concrete. What happens after a covered event, what happens if cash is short today, and what will support commitments in later years?
